Defining Noise vs. Outliers in Train Delays

Noise: Noise is a random error or variance in a measured variable. In your dataset, this could be caused by faulty data collection instruments, data entry problems, or transmission errors. For example, a typographical error logging a 1,000,000-minute delay is garbage data that must be smoothed or removed. As a general rule in data mining, noise should be removed before outlier detection to prevent it from blurring the distinction between normal objects and true anomalies.  

Outliers: An outlier is a data object that deviates significantly from the rest of the dataset, appearing as if it were generated by a different mechanism.   

The Project Context: In anomaly mining, "one person’s garbage could be another person’s treasure". While a 14-hour delay deviates wildly from the mean, it is likely an exceptional treasure indicating a severe structural bottleneck rather than a random error. Our goal is to preserve these genuine extremes while smoothing out the impossible transmission errors.

Since the dataset was scraped from an Indian Railways tracking platform like etrain.info, you are absolutely right to question classifying them as random noise. In these specific public tracking systems, negative values are intentionally used to log early arrivals. A recorded delay of -15 physically means the train arrived 15 minutes ahead of its scheduled timetable.

In Indian Railways operations and tracking systems like etrain.info (which sources from the National Train Enquiry System), a 24-hour (1,440-minute) delay is the standard operational cutoff.

When a train is delayed beyond 24 hours, Indian Railways typically officially "reschedules" the journey to a new departure date or cancels it entirely. This is a physical necessity: if a daily train is delayed by more than 24 hours, it begins to conflict with the next day's scheduled instance of that exact same train.

Therefore, any continuous delay recorded above 1,440 minutes is generally a transmission glitch, a rollover error from the scraping system, or a formally rescheduled train that should not be evaluated as a standard physical delay.

2 Standard Deviations (The High-Risk Zone): This boundary identifies stations experiencing significant, systemic congestion. These delays fall outside the normal 95% of operations and help you map the broader geographical and structural stress points across railway zones.   

3 Standard Deviations (The Critical Zone): This stricter boundary isolates the absolute worst structural failures. These represent the top 0.3% of delays and highlight the truly "critical transit stations" that consistently trigger massive, cascading network delays.

The Physics of Railway Speed (The Left Wall): Train delays are heavily right-skewed. A structural failure can easily delay a train by 15 hours, but a train physically cannot arrive 15 hours early due to mechanical speed limits and distance. Mathematically, 2 standard deviations below your true mean is -98.1 minutes ($35.6 - (2 \times 66.9)$). Because we already confirmed your absolute maximum early arrival is exactly -120 minutes, there is no long "negative tail" hiding massive anomalies. The data hits a strict physical wall, meaning standard deviation is only a useful anomaly-detection tool on the boundless positive side.
